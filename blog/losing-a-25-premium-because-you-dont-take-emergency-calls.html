Losing a 25% Premium Because You Don't Take Emergency Calls

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · September 29, 2026 · 5 min read

The call comes in at 9 p.m. No heat. Water on the floor. A breaker panel that smells like something burning. The homeowner is scared, he is motivated, and he will pay almost anything to make the problem stop tonight. If that call rings out to voicemail, you did not just miss a job. You missed the most profitable version of that job. The customer who was ready to pay extra is already on the phone with the shop that picked up.

The premium hiding in the after-hours call

Emergency work is not priced like a Tuesday afternoon tune-up. It carries a premium, and the customer knows it going in. Industry averages suggest after-hours and emergency service runs 1.5 to 2 times your standard rate, or a markup of 25% to 50% on the ticket. Nobody calls at 9 p.m. to price shop. They call because the pain is real right now, and stopping it is worth more to them than the number on the invoice.

The math on one call you don't answer

A homeowner calls at 9 p.m. with a burst supply line. The repair itself, the base job, runs about $450. Because it is after hours, that same job carries a premium. At a 33% markup, call it another $150 on top. So the ticket is worth around $600 tonight.

Two numbers live inside that one call. Look at them apart:

  • The base job: $450. The work you would have done at any hour.
  • The premium: $150. The extra the clock earns you, for the same truck and the same repair.

Miss the call and you do not lose one or the other. You lose both. The base job walks, and the premium that rode on top of it walks with it.

Now annualize it

One missed call is $600. That rarely happens just once. Say only 3 emergency calls a week roll to voicemail. Here is what leaves the building in a year:

  • The premium alone: $150 a call, 3 a week, 50 weeks. That is $22,500 a year.
  • The base jobs: $450 a call, same math. That is $67,500 a year.
  • The two together: $90,000 a year.

That is the modest version. A busier shop losing 5 or 6 of these a week is well past $150,000. Run your own average ticket and your own after-hours call count. The number is almost always bigger than the owner guessed.

Why "we'll call them back in the morning" is the leak

By morning the emergency is over. Someone else came out, fixed it, and got paid the premium. And most of those callers never gave you a second chance to begin with. Industry studies commonly find that around 80% of people who reach a voicemail hang up without leaving a message. So the after-hours call does not sit in your inbox waiting on your first cup of coffee. It is gone, with no trace it ever happened.

Speed is the whole game here. Research on how buyers pick a company consistently shows that about half of them go with the first business to respond, and some studies put that as high as 78%. The homeowner with water on the floor is not comparing three quotes. He is calling down the list and stopping at the first live voice.

Put those two together. Say 6 emergency calls hit your voicemail in a week. Around 80% leave no message, so 5 of the 6 vanish without a sound. Answer live instead and you become that first responder. Capture even half of those calls, the way the speed research says you would, and that is 3 booked jobs a week you were throwing away. At $600 each, you just recovered that $90,000 a year.

How to catch the call without living on your phone

You do not have to answer every ring yourself. That is not the fix, and it is the fastest way to burn out. The fix is a decision. Decide who covers after-hours. Decide what gets quoted and what gets scheduled. Then make sure every call is answered live by a real voice, every time, so the motivated customer never hits a dead end.

Start by counting. Track how many calls come in after you close for 30 days, and what happens to each one. The leak turns into a number the moment you measure it.

Common questions

Do I have to answer every emergency call myself to capture this?

No. Owners who try that end up exhausted and still miss calls. What you need is a setup where every after-hours call is answered live, triaged, and either booked or routed. Who picks up matters far less than the fact that someone does.

What is one after-hours call actually worth?

Both halves of the ticket. On a $450 emergency repair, an industry-average premium of 25% to 50% adds roughly $110 to $225 on top. Miss the call and you lose the base job and the premium together. A few a week runs near $90,000 a year for a typical shop.

We are not a 24/7 operation. Does this still apply?

Yes. This is not about being open all night. It is about the call being answered when it comes in, so a ready-to-pay customer books with you instead of the next name on his list.
