A shared lead that costs you $50 and closes 1 time in 10 costs you $500 for every booked job, and you pay that before a single truck leaves the yard. Say you run 15 of those a month. You are spending somewhere between $6,000 and $18,000 a year to rent work you could be winning for close to nothing. The platform calls it a lead. What you are buying is a footrace against 3 or 4 other shops for a customer who may never pick up the phone.

The number they tell you, and the number that is true

One owner got a paid-lead platform on the phone and asked a plain question: how many pros get my lead? The answer was 3. So she ran the test live, posing as a customer. The system showed her 5 contractors, then texted her 5 more. One of her own customers later told her that 21 pros had received his job. In her words: "at least 21 Angi pros received his lead, when Angi is telling you only 3 contractors receive a customer's lead."

Think about what that does to your close rate. If the platform quietly sends the same job to 20 shops, you are a long way down the list by the time you dial. The customer has already talked to someone, already has a number in his head, and already quit answering unknown callers. One contractor summed up the position in six words: "You're not the first callback. You're the fourth." You paid full price for a lead that got sold down the street 20 more times.

You pay whether you win or not

Here is the part that should bother you. On most of these platforms, you are charged for the lead, not the job. You pay to enter the race, and there is no refund when you lose it. The Federal Trade Commission's April 2023 final consent order against HomeAdvisor and Angi Leads ran up to $7.2 million over how those leads were sold. The company settled without admitting the allegations, and pros still pay for leads whether or not they ever win the work.

In our research from owners and operators this fall, anger at paid-lead platforms was one of the loudest, most-repeated complaints we heard. One painting contractor put real numbers on it: around $350 spent in a single month on 7 or 8 leads, and zero actual customers to show for it. New platform, same math. "Paid leads are a gamble," one owner wrote. He is right. A gamble is a fine way to spend a slow afternoon. It is a poor way to fill a schedule.

Count the cost per booked job, not the cost per lead

The fix starts with one honest number. Stop counting what a lead costs. Count what a booked job costs you. Take everything you spent on a platform last month and divide it by the jobs it actually produced. At $50 a lead and a 10% close rate, that is $500 a booked job before you have done any work. A lead that 8 of 10 buyers never answer barely counts as a lead. Once an owner sees that number written down, the spend usually stops looking so clever.

Then look at the work you already paid for and never chased. The customer you served 2 years ago and never called again. The estimate you sent and let go cold. The caller who hit voicemail at 6 p.m. and dialed the next name. Those people already know you, and they cost you almost nothing to reach. Winning one of them back is cheaper than any lead you will ever buy, and you are sitting on a whole list of them right now.

What to do this week

Pull your last 3 months of platform spend and the jobs it actually booked. Divide one by the other, and write that cost-per-booked-job number where you will see it every day. Then pull your last 50 invoices, pick 10 customers you have not spoken to in a year, and call them. That is one afternoon, and it will tell you more about where your next job comes from than another month of bought leads ever will.

Common questions

Are paid-lead platforms always a bad deal?

No. A brand-new shop with no reputation and an empty calendar sometimes has to buy its way to the first few jobs. The trouble starts when an established shop keeps renting its work flow long after word of mouth and repeat customers should be carrying the load. Years in business and still living on bought leads is the leak.

How do I know if I am overpaying?

Run the cost-per-booked-job math above. If a booked job costs you more in lead fees than a past customer would cost to win back, you are overpaying, and that gap is money you can recover.

What is the cheapest lead I can get?

The customer you already served. Reviews, a current Google profile, and a short call to a past customer cost almost nothing and reach people who already trust you. Build that first, then decide whether you still need to rent leads at all.
