The Estimate Black Hole

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · November 5, 2026 · 4 min read

You sent the estimate. It got opened. Then nothing. No yes, no no, no reply at all. A home-service shop that loses one job a week to that silence hands back somewhere between $40,000 and $125,000 a year. Those are built numbers for illustration, drawn from field-typical inputs: one job a week, at a ticket between $800 and $2,500, across a working year. Round them down if you want. The figure stays big, because the leak runs every week the phone keeps ringing.

The silence runs both ways

Here is the part most owners never see. The silence that stings you is the same silence you hand your customers.

Owners say it plain. "Sent the estimate as normal got viewed. Then never heard back." Homeowners, standing on the other side of that same driveway, say it too. "No quote. No call. No explanation." "They said they would send it over immediately. 2 days passed and nothing." One of them waited two weeks, gave up, and left a 1-star review on the way out the door.

Kestrel tracked 20 posts across Facebook Groups, Facebook, Instagram and Threads between September 27 and October 26, 2026 — 9 of 20 (45%) named estimate follow-up failure or ghosting, across 154 comments counted on 16 of the 20 posts. Owners ghosted on one side. Buyers ghosted on the other. Same leak, two ends of the same pipe.

So ask yourself one question. How many of your own open estimates got a second touch from you this week?

The drive you already paid for

Walk the math on a single estimate. Call it 45 minutes of drive time, an hour measuring and talking it through, and two hours writing it up clean. Four hours, and not one of them billed.

When the estimate dies in silence, the job is gone and so are the hours. You drove out, you measured, you wrote it up, and all four hours walk out the door with it. Do that four times a month and you have burned two full workdays on quotes nobody ever even turned down.

Why your books never flag it

A job that was never booked leaves no hole in a report. There is no line item called "quotes that went quiet." Your bookkeeper never sees it. You feel busy, because you are. You drove, you measured, you wrote. The work that walked out the door looks exactly like a slow week.

That is how the busiest stretch of your year can feel like the thinnest. The leak does its damage in the gap between "sent" and "signed," where nobody is watching.

Close the gap on purpose

The fix is a simple rhythm you hold on every estimate, whether you feel like it or not.

  • Deliver the estimate, or give a firm date for it, within 48 hours of the visit. Speed is the edge your competitor keeps handing you.
  • Touch it again on day 2 and day 5, and say something new each time instead of "just checking in."
  • Keep one list of every open estimate with the date of its last touch. If you cannot see it, you cannot chase it.
  • Let the follow-up run on a system instead of your memory, so it still happens on the day you are buried on a roof.

That last one is where the quiet shops pull ahead. There is a way to make every estimate get its day-2 and day-5 touch without you holding any of it in your head. That part is worth a conversation.

Common questions

How fast does an estimate really have to go out?

Faster than your competitor's, and the bar is low. Most quotes take about two days. Beat that and you are often the first real number in the customer's hands, which is usually the one that wins.

I am slammed. When am I supposed to chase old estimates?

You are not, by hand. The chase is a process triggered by a date. You build it once, and it runs while you are on the truck.

Will following up too much annoy people?

A touch that says something useful reads as service. The homeowners above were not angry that someone followed up. They were angry that nobody did.
