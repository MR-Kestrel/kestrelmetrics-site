The Quote That Vanished: Why Follow-Up Is Where Contractors Lose the Most Money

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · July 23, 2026 · 4 min read

The single most expensive habit in home services is sending a quote and never following up on it. Industry averages suggest most sales close after several contacts, yet the typical contractor stops after one. That gap quietly costs many owners somewhere between $2,000 and $8,000 a month in work that was already estimated, already priced, and nearly signed. The job did not go to a better bid. It went to whoever called back.

The quote was the hard part, and you already did it

By the time you send a number, you have driven out, looked at the work, and spent your time. That is the expensive part of winning a job, and you have already paid it. Then most owners let the quote sit and go silent, waiting for the customer to call. Half the time the customer meant to say yes and got busy. The other half took your number to a competitor who followed up. Either way the quote vanished, and so did the money in it.

Silence is not a no

When a customer goes quiet after a quote, owners read it as a rejection and move on. It usually is not. People get distracted, compare options, and wait for a nudge. Industry averages suggest a large share of quoted jobs that never close simply never got a second touch. A short call or text two days later, then again a week later, recovers a real slice of them. You are not being pushy. You are finishing what you started.

What one missing follow-up costs

Run the numbers on your own shop. Take the quotes you sent last month that went nowhere. Put your average job value on them. Now assume a steady follow-up would win back even 1 in 5. That recovered fifth is pure found money, because you already did the site visit and the estimate. For most contractors that one habit is worth more than any new ad campaign, and it costs nothing but the reminder.

Why it does not happen

Follow-up dies because it lives in someone's memory, and memories are busy. The tech is on the next job. The owner is buried. The quote is in a text thread three days deep. Nothing is broken on purpose. The system just forgets, and forgetting is the leak. Fix it with a simple rule: every quote gets a scheduled second and third touch, tracked somewhere that is not a person's head.

Common questions

How many times should I follow up on a quote?

At least two or three times over a week or two. Most jobs that close after the first quote close on a later touch. Stopping at one leaves the majority of the recoverable work on the table.

Does follow-up annoy customers?

A short, useful check-in rarely does. Most people who went quiet simply got busy and appreciate the nudge. The ones who were never going to buy will tell you, and now you know instead of wondering.

What is the easiest way to make sure it happens?

Take it out of memory. Every quote gets a scheduled reminder or an automated message so the follow-up fires whether or not anyone remembers. The habit only works when it does not depend on a busy person recalling it.

You did not lose the bid. You stopped talking, and the job went to the one who did not.
