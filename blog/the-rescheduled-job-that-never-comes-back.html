The Rescheduled Job That Never Comes Back

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · October 23, 2026 · 5 min read

A cancellation with a new date on it is a delay. A cancellation with no new date is a slow goodbye. The customer already picked you. The quote is signed, the material is bought and riding in the truck, and the only thing left is to put a day on the calendar. Then the call comes in. "Something came up, let me get back to you on timing." You say no problem. You mean it. And that job just started dying.

Most owners never count this one, because on paper nothing was lost. The job is still "open." It just never closes. For a shop your size, that open-ended pile quietly costs $2,500 to $8,000 a month in work you already earned. Here is where it goes.

How a booked job quietly goes cold

An open-ended reschedule has no owner. The customer is not going to call you back; he has moved on to whatever came up. The tech assumes the office will chase it. The office assumes the customer will call when he is ready. The material sits on the truck or the shelf with his name on it, tying up cash you already spent.

Every day that gap stays open, the job cools. The urgency that made him sign fades. A competitor's truck rolls down his street. The window you already won slides shut while everyone waits for someone else to make the call.

Why the delay itself is the leak

The delay is doing the damage all on its own.

Research across millions of appointments found that the longer the gap before a visit, the more likely it gets abandoned. A wait of more than a month made customers more than twice as likely to cancel and never rebook, compared with those seen inside a week. The clock works against you a little more every day the job sits.

And service work starts from a rough spot to begin with. Industry averages suggest 10% to 30% of booked service appointments fall through as no-shows or cancellations, with home services sitting near the high end of that range. An open-ended reschedule takes a job that was already at risk and removes the one thing holding it in place: a date.

What it costs, counted out

Run this on your own shop tonight.

Say 15 jobs a month get pushed to an open-ended date. If even a third of them never come back, that is 5 finished jobs you lost. At an average ticket of $500 to $1,500, that is $2,500 to $7,500 a month. Annualized, you are looking at $30,000 to $90,000 a year in work that was already sold, quoted, and sitting in your truck.

That is only the job. It does not count the parts you now have to restock or eat, or the customer who tells three neighbors you "never followed up."

The fix is a cadence the office owns

The reason these jobs die is that the next step lives in someone's memory, and memory loses to a busy week every time. The recovery is a simple, repeating cadence that does not depend on anyone remembering: a reminder when the date slips, a follow-up a few days later with a specific day offered, and a confirmation the moment a new date is set.

This is some of the easiest revenue in your building, and the numbers back it up. One study of text reminders cut missed appointments by almost 40%. Automated reminder sequences in other studies cut them by around 29%. Put a process on your open-ended reschedules and you recover a real share of that pile for the cost of a few messages. Save even two jobs a month and the system has paid for itself many times over.

The money here is already yours. It is work you booked and paid to stock, sitting one phone call away from done.

Common questions

How is an open-ended reschedule different from a normal cancellation?

A normal cancellation is clean. The customer is out, and you refill the slot. An open-ended reschedule is worse, because it looks alive. It stays on your books as a maybe, so nobody refills the slot and nobody chases it. That is exactly how it hides.

We do not have that many. Is it worth building a process for?

Run your own number first. Five lost jobs a month at $800 each is $4,000, and the same leak runs every month you leave it open. A reminder cadence costs almost nothing to set up and recovers most of those. There are not many fixes in your shop with that kind of return.

What does the process actually look like?

Three touches the office owns, not the tech: a reminder when a job goes open-ended, a follow-up a few days later with a specific date offered, and a confirmation as soon as he picks one. Every open-ended job gets a status and a next-touch date on the board, so none of them can quietly go cold.
