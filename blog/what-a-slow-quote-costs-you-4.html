What a Slow Quote Costs You

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · October 8, 2026 · 5 min read

You drove out Tuesday. You walked the yard, climbed the attic, or pulled the panel. You told the homeowner you would get him a number. You did, on Friday, after the week let up. By then he had two other quotes in hand and one of those companies was already on his calendar. You did the same work to win that job as the shop that got it. You just finished three days later.

The first number in the door usually wins

A homeowner asking for a quote is not shopping for fun. He has a problem in front of him and he wants it gone. Most collect about three quotes, and they do not wait for all three before they start deciding. Industry studies put the share of buyers who go with the first company to respond as high as 78%. A widely cited Harvard Business Review study of more than 2 million leads found that companies waiting a full day to follow up were 60 times less likely to get anywhere with that customer than those who responded within the hour.

Meanwhile, the average business takes close to two days to reply at all. That gap is your opening. You do not have to be the cheapest shop in town or the biggest. You have to be the one whose number shows up while the customer still cares.

The math on a slow quote

Say your shop sends 6 quotes a week. Over a 50-week year that is 300 quotes. Contractor data suggests a quote that lands the same day closes around 40% of the time, and one that lands two days later closes closer to 25%.

Put a number on the difference:

  • Same-day quotes: 300 quotes at 40% is 120 jobs.
  • Two-day quotes: 300 quotes at 25% is 75 jobs.
  • The gap: 45 jobs at a $1,500 average, or $67,500 a year.

Same leads. Same site visits. Same fuel, same hours, same prices. The only thing that changed was when the number arrived. Run it with your own quote count and your own average job. A shop with a $2,500 ticket is past $100,000.

Why a fast quote makes a happy customer

Speed is not just a race. It tells the customer something about you before you ever swing a hammer.

He is judging the whole company off the one thing he can see so far. He cannot inspect your workmanship yet. He can see how long you took to get back to him. A quote that shows up the same evening says you are organized, you want the work, and you will show up when you say you will. A quote that shows up Friday says the opposite, and no line item fixes that.

It also ends his worry sooner. A leaking roof or a dead AC unit is stress he carries until somebody gives him a price and a plan. The shop that hands him both tonight took a weight off him. He expected to wait days, because that is what every other contractor has taught him to expect. When you beat that, he notices, and he tells you so with a signature.

Speed comes from process, not hustle

You do not fix a slow quote by working later. Working later is how the quote got to Friday in the first place. You fix it by building a process that makes a fast, accurate quote the normal result, not a good week.

That takes two things working together.

A defined workflow. Write down every step from the first call to the sent quote, for your shop and your kind of work. What gets asked on the first call. What gets measured and photographed on site, in the same order, every time. Who builds the number, who signs off, and how long each step is allowed to sit. If the quote depends on what the owner remembers from a walk-through, it will be slow and it will be wrong. If it depends on a checklist anyone on your crew can follow, it is repeatable.

The right technology. A price book so nobody builds a number from scratch. Quote templates for your most common jobs. Photos and measurements captured on a phone at the site, not rebuilt from memory at the kitchen table. A tool that sends the quote and tells you when he opened it.

Neither one works alone. Software on top of a sloppy process just sends bad numbers faster. A good process with no tools still waits on the owner's evening. Together they get you a quote that is fast, consistent, and accurate, and accurate matters as much as fast. A quick number you have to walk back costs you more trust than a slow one.

Start by timing it. For the next 30 days, write down when you saw each job and when the quote went out. That gap is your speed to quote, and most owners have never measured it.

Common questions

How fast is fast enough?

Same day is the target and 24 hours is the line. If you can hand him a number before you leave the driveway, do it. The quote that goes out tonight beats the better quote that goes out Friday.

Won't a faster quote be a less accurate one?

Only if speed comes from rushing. When it comes from a price book, a site checklist, and a template, the fast quote is the more accurate one, because nothing is being rebuilt from memory two days later.

We are slammed. How do we quote faster without hiring?

Take the quote off the owner's plate. Most owners are the bottleneck because they build every number themselves between jobs. A written workflow and a price book let the office or a crew lead send it the same day, and the owner only signs off.
