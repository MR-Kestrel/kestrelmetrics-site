What a Technician Leaves on the Table Every Visit

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · July 24, 2026 · 4 min read

A technician who notices a problem and does not put a number on it can walk away from $200 to $900 in work on a single visit. The truck is already parked out front. The customer already trusts the person standing in the hallway. Industry averages suggest the repair or replacement your tech saw, but never quoted, is the highest-margin dollar of the whole day, and most of it drives off with the van.

The truck roll is already paid for

By the time your tech is standing in a customer's garage, you have already spent the money to get him there. Dispatch, drive time, fuel, and the hour of labor run most home services businesses somewhere between $50 and $300 before a wrench turns. That cost is spent whether he sells one repair or three.

So the second finding on that visit costs you almost nothing to surface. No new lead. No new ad. No new drive. It is the cheapest revenue you will ever see, and it is sitting in the same house your tech is already standing in.

The three things a tech sees and never says

Walk any experienced technician through a service call and he will notice things the customer will not. The water heater that is eleven years old and weeping at the base. The capacitor reading low that will strand the homeowner in August. The maintenance the customer skipped two years running.

None of that is invented work. It is real condition, seen by a trained set of eyes. The leak is not that your techs miss it. The leak is that they see it, keep it in their head, and drive to the next call without ever putting it in front of the person who could say yes.

Why the money walks

This is a process gap, not a people problem. You hired your techs to fix things, and they are good at it. Nobody handed them a standard for what happens when they spot a second issue. No photo. No written estimate. No number on paper before they leave.

So the finding lives in the tech's memory until the drive home, and then it is gone. The customer never got the chance to decide, because they never knew there was a decision to make. Industry averages suggest that in trades like HVAC and plumbing, a large share of visits carry at least one condition worth flagging, and only a fraction of those ever become a written quote.

What it counts up to

Run the arithmetic on your own board. Say your crew runs 40 service calls in a week. If one in four carries a second finding worth an average of $400, that is 10 quotes and $4,000 of work that existed and was seen. Capture half and you bank $2,000 a week you were already close enough to touch. Over a year that is real money that never showed up in any ad report, because it was never a lead problem.

Adjust the numbers to your own ticket and volume. The point holds at any size: the truck was already there.

How you see it

You cannot count what nobody writes down. The fix starts with one rule: every visit ends with a written estimate for anything the tech recommends, even when the customer is going to say no today. Photograph the finding. Put a number on it. Track recommended dollars against sold dollars, tech by tech.

Once you can see the gap between what was spotted and what was sold, the money stops being invisible. You are no longer guessing. You are watching a number you can move.

Common questions

Is this just teaching my techs to upsell junk?

No. Upselling junk is inventing work that does not need doing. This is putting a number on real conditions your tech already sees and would mention to his own mother. The customer still decides. You are only making sure they get to.

How do I know this is happening in my shop?

Ask for last week's recommended work versus sold work, tech by tech. If you cannot produce that number, that is the answer. The gap is there; it is just not being counted.

What is the fastest way to start recovering it?

One rule, starting Monday: no visit closes without a written estimate for anything the tech flags, plus a photo. You will see the recovered dollars inside a single billing cycle, with no new spend.
