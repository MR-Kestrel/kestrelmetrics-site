Why Your Best Customers Only Call You Once

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · August 28, 2026 · 4 min read

A one-time customer is worth the ticket in front of you, somewhere around $200 to $600. That same customer, kept for five to ten years, is worth $2,000 to $5,000 or more in repeat work and the referrals that come with it. Industry averages suggest you spent five to seven times more to win that customer than you would have spent to keep them. When a good customer only calls you once, the gap between those two numbers is money you already earned and let go cold.

The first job is the most expensive one you will ever do for them

Think about what it took to win that customer the first time. The ad spend. The call your office answered. The truck you sent. The quote, the trip, the hour of labor before you ever billed a dollar. By the time you finished the job and got paid, that first ticket carried the whole cost of finding them.

The second job carries almost none of it. They already have your number. They already trust the work. Getting them to call again costs a reminder and a few minutes. That is the whole point of the first job, and it is the part most owners never collect on.

Where the customer goes cold

Here is how a good customer quietly turns into a stranger. You finish the work. You get paid. Nobody follows up. No thank-you, no maintenance reminder, no note when the season turns.

Six months later their water heater goes out, or their unit stops cooling, and they do what everybody does now. They pull out their phone and type your trade plus their town into the search bar. You were already in that phone. They just did not see you, so they called whoever showed up first. You did the hard part and handed the easy part to a competitor.

Count what a repeat customer is actually worth

Run the numbers on one house. Say the first job was $400. That same house needs you again every year or two for a repair, a tune-up, a part. Over ten years that is a handful of visits, and industry averages put a retained home services customer somewhere in the low thousands before you count a single referral.

Now add the referral. A customer who trusts you sends a neighbor, a sister, a guy from church. One kept customer can be worth two or three more you never had to advertise for. A one-time job is a number you can see on the invoice. A kept customer is a number sitting off the books, and it is usually the bigger one.

The follow-up you are not doing

Staying in view is not a campaign. It is a habit. A short message after the job. A reminder before the season they will need you. A once-a-year touch so your name is the one already in the phone when something breaks.

None of it is complicated. It is the kind of thing that falls off the truck because the day is full and nobody owns it. Trace the trail of your last hundred customers and count how many you have heard from twice. The ones you have not heard from did not leave because the work was bad. They left because nothing ever brought them back.

Common questions

How much more does it cost to win a new customer than to keep one I already served?

Industry averages suggest acquiring a new customer runs five to seven times the cost of keeping an existing one. The first job absorbs the ad spend, the office time, and the trip to win them. A repeat job from someone already in your system costs a reminder and a few minutes.

What is a repeat home services customer actually worth?

A single ticket might be $200 to $600. The same customer kept over five to ten years, across repairs, tune-ups, and the referrals they send, commonly reaches the low thousands. The one-time number shows up on the invoice. The lifetime number sits off the books.

How often should I reach out to a past customer?

Enough to stay in plain sight. A message after the job, a reminder before the season they will need you, and a light touch once a year is usually enough to be the name already in their phone when something breaks.

You paid full price to win them once. Letting them go cold means paying it again for the next stranger who could have been them.
