You Haven't Raised Your Prices, and It's Costing You

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · October 22, 2026 · 5 min read

Most shops that have held their prices flat for two or three years are giving back $25,000 to $90,000 a year in margin. This leak hides inside the jobs you win. The job still books. The truck still rolls. You just keep less of every dollar than you did two years ago, and nobody sent you a bill for the difference.

Your costs moved. Your price didn't.

Think about what you paid two years ago for a tech's hour, a tank of fuel, a box of parts. Now look at what you pay today. Industry averages suggest those costs have climbed a good deal since 2023, and every owner feels it. The part that hides is the other side of the ledger. Your price list mostly stayed where it was.

Here is the trap. Raising a price feels like a risky move, so you put it off. Holding a price steady feels safe, so it keeps happening by default. But standing pat while your costs climb is its own quiet decision, and it costs you on every invoice you send.

What it actually costs

Run the rough math on your own shop. Say your costs have risen 6% to 8% since you last set your rates. On a job you bill at $100, you are keeping $6 to $8 less than you used to, every time you send it. On one ticket it barely registers.

Now stretch it across a year. A few points of margin on $1,000,000 of work is $30,000 to $50,000 a year off your bottom line. On a few million in revenue, multiply it. You earned that money and then handed it right back, because the price on the invoice was set in a world that no longer exists.

And it compounds the other direction too. Every year you hold prices flat while costs climb, the gap gets wider, and the raise you eventually have to make gets bigger and scarier. The longer you wait, the harder the fix.

Why you cannot see it

This leak hides because the top line looks fine. Revenue is steady. Jobs are closing. The bank account is still moving. The damage sits down in the margin, where the top line never shows it. Your profit-and-loss shows the costs went up, because receipts arrive in the mail. It stays silent on the raise you never took, because a price you never charged leaves no paper trail.

A client of mine was sure his pricing was current. When we traced his average cost per job against the price he was charging, he had absorbed almost three years of cost increases without a single rate change. He was working harder than ever and wondering why the profit felt thinner. The work was solid. The price was three years stale, and it was costing him on every job that went out the door.

The fear, and the answer to it

Here is what stops most owners. "If I raise my prices, I will lose customers." That fear is real, and it is worth taking seriously. So take it seriously with a number instead of a guess.

  • Raise in measured steps, on a schedule you set ahead of time. An increase that tracks your real cost rise just puts you back where you should have been all along.
  • Know your worth before you quote it. A customer who only stays for the lowest price was never really yours, and keeping him at a loss is its own leak.
  • Lead with the value of the work. You are charging what it actually costs to do the job right, and that is a number you can stand behind.

The owners who hold the line on price are often the ones losing the most, because they are the ones most afraid to look at the number.

Common questions

How do I know if this is happening in my shop?

Pull your average cost per job from two or three years ago and compare it with today. Then compare your prices across the same stretch. If your costs moved and your prices did not, the gap is your leak, and it is landing on every invoice.

Won't raising prices cost me customers?

Some, maybe. The question is which ones. A measured increase that matches your rising costs usually keeps the customers who value the work and sheds the ones who only chase the lowest number. Holding prices flat to keep everyone is how you keep the least profitable customers and starve the business that serves the good ones.

How much should I raise them?

Enough to cover what your costs have actually done, at a minimum. The exact number comes out of your own books, so dig it out of your real labor, fuel, and parts costs. Most owners are surprised how far behind they have let it drift.
