You're already a contractor. Now you're a lender.

By Michael Reaves · Kestrel Metrics · November 17, 2026 · 5 min read

You priced the job, bought the material, paid your crew on Friday, and closed the ticket. The customer had the finished work the day it was done. You had an invoice stamped net 60. Somewhere in that gap you quietly became a lender, and nobody is paying you interest on the loan. Our research from owners/operators across Facebook groups, Instagram, and Threads keeps landing on the same wound: unpaid invoices, deposit no-shows, and payment that never got collected. The money you earned is working for everybody but you.

What the waiting actually costs

Say you bill around $250,000 a month. On net 60 terms, roughly two months of finished work sits on the street at any given moment. That is about $500,000 of your money funding other people's operations. Carry that half-million at a conservative 10% cost of money, the rate a line of credit or a missed investment would run you, and the waiting alone costs $45,000 to $50,000 a year. That is the price of lending customers their own project.

Then there is the money that never comes home at all. Industry averages suggest a contractor never collects 1% to 3% of everything he bills. On $3M, that is $30,000 to $90,000 gone. One owner put it plainly: "We did the work. We paid our employees. We met every deadline. But over two years later, we're still waiting to get paid."

Why it never shows up in your books

A leak like this hides because it is not a receipt. Your bookkeeper sees expenses and a revenue number that looks healthy. What she cannot see is that a big slice of that revenue is parked in somebody else's account, aging. You feel it the day you cover payroll out of reserves while $80,000 you already earned sits unpaid. The books say you had a good month. Your bank balance says you floated the whole town.

The deposit side of the same hole

The other half of this leak opens before the work even starts. You order material, load the truck, and roll a crew on the strength of a handshake. Collect nothing up front and you have funded the start of that job yourself, then spend the back end chasing the balance. This runs deep on the owner side. Our research from owners/operators across Reddit tells the same story: unpaid invoices, refused payment, and the customer who never showed. The lesson runs both directions. Money should change hands before the first day on site, and again the day the work is done. The gap in between is the leak.

How to close it this week

The fix is a short list you can start on your next quote.

  • Put the terms in writing before anyone swings a hammer: a deposit due to book, the balance due on completion, in plain language the customer signs.
  • Collect a deposit or a milestone before day one. A deductible, a booking fee, or a percentage down turns a handshake into a commitment.
  • Offer two prices: the number due on completion, and a higher number after 14 days. Or frame it from the other side: a lower price, offered as a discount, for paying early. Either way, paying on time is the cheaper choice.
  • The moment a payment or a start date slips, send a dated text or email the same day. A phone call you forget leaves no paper. A time-stamped message builds the trail that gets you paid.

Most of it runs on its own once set up, so the reminders go out whether you remember them or not.

Common questions

Is net 30 or net 60 just the cost of doing business in the trades?

It is common. Common still costs you. Every day of terms you grant is a day you finance the customer. If a general contractor or a restoration company demands net 60, price that cost into the job the way you price fuel and insurance, so the waiting is paid for by the work.

My customers are good people. Won't a deposit insult them?

A good customer has no problem putting money down on work he wants done. A deposit is simply how the job gets funded, so you carry it out of his pocket instead of yours. The ones who balk at a reasonable deposit are telling you something useful before you have a dollar at risk.

What do I do about the invoices already aging out there?

Start a paper trail today. A short, dated message on every past-due balance, then a firmer one on a set schedule. The oldest money is the hardest to collect, so work it first and put every touch in writing.
